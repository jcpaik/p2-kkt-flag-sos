# Mathematical background

This document develops the certificate search from first principles.

## 1. Lines as points of the projective plane

A line through the origin in \(\mathbb R^3\) can be represented by a unit
vector \(x\in S^2\), but \(x\) and \(-x\) represent the same line. The space of
such unoriented lines is the real projective plane

\[
\mathbb{RP}^2=S^2/\{x\sim -x\}.
\]

Because the kernel studied here is even, \(K(t)=K(-t)\), its value on two
projective lines is well defined by \(K(x\cdot y)\), independently of the
chosen signs of \(x\) and \(y\).

A probability measure \(\mu\) on \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) describes a possibly
continuous distribution of lines. Equivalently, it can be represented by an
antipodally symmetric probability measure on \(S^2\).

## 2. Energy and copositivity

For a continuous even kernel \(K:[-1,1]\to\mathbb R\), define

\[
E_K(\mu)
=
\iint K(x\cdot y)\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y).
\]

The kernel is called **copositive** on \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) if

\[
E_K(\mu)\ge0
\]

for every finite nonnegative measure \(\mu\). By homogeneity, it is enough to
check probability measures.

Copositivity is weaker than positive definiteness. A positive-definite zonal
kernel has nonnegative coefficients in its spherical-harmonic expansion.
The present kernel has a negative degree-four harmonic coefficient, so the
usual two-point positive-definite-kernel argument cannot apply directly.

The kernel in this repository is

\[
K(t)=32t^6-48t^4+20t^2-\frac43.
\tag{2.1}
\]

It arises from the trigonometric expression

\[
\cos(2\theta)+\cos(6\theta)+\frac23,
\qquad |t|=\cos\theta.
\]

For an orthonormal basis \(e_1,e_2,e_3\), the measure

\[
\mu_{\mathrm{ONB}}
=
\frac13(\delta_{e_1}+\delta_{e_2}+\delta_{e_3})
\]

has zero energy. Thus any proof of copositivity must be sharp.

## 3. Pair moments and isotropy

Write

\[
p_j(\mu)=\iint(x\cdot y)^j\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y).
\]

Equation (2.1) gives

\[
E_K(\mu)=32p_6-48p_4+20p_2-\frac43.
\tag{3.1}
\]

The second-moment matrix is

\[
M_2(\mu)=\int xx^T\,d\mu(x).
\]

The measure is **isotropic** if

\[
M_2(\mu)=\frac13I.
\tag{3.2}
\]

Since \(p_2=\operatorname{tr}(M_2^2)\) and \(\operatorname{tr}M_2=1\), one
always has \(p_2\ge1/3\), with equality precisely at isotropy. The
difference is a harmonic square:

\[
h_2=\frac{3p_2-1}{2}=\sum_m|\hat\mu_{2m}|^2
=\frac32\left\|M_2-\tfrac13I\right\|_F^2\ \ge\ 0.
\]

The code makes **no isotropy assumption**. It uses the general normalized
target

\[
T(\mu)=-\frac14+\frac{15}{4}p_2-9p_4+6p_6=\frac{3}{16}E_K(\mu),
\tag{3.3}
\]

keeps \(p_2\) and every other antipodally even moment as an independent
label, and encodes the isotropy deficit through the flag squares
\(h_2\ge0\) and the spin-2 Gram blocks of Section 6.

## 4. The potential and measure KKT conditions

Define the potential generated by \(\mu\):

\[
U_\mu(z)=\int K(z\cdot y)\,d\mu(y).
\]

Then

\[
E_K(\mu)=\int U_\mu(x)\,d\mu(x).
\]

Suppose \(\mu\) is a global minimizer of \(E_K\) over probability measures.
For a trial point \(z\), consider

\[
\mu_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)\mu+\varepsilon\delta_z.
\]

Differentiating at \(\varepsilon=0\) gives

\[
\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}E_K(\mu_\varepsilon)\right|_{\varepsilon=0}
=2\bigl(U_\mu(z)-E_K(\mu)\bigr).
\]

Minimality therefore implies the global first-variation inequality

\[
q_\mu(z):=U_\mu(z)-E_K(\mu)\ge0
\qquad\text{for every }z.
\tag{4.1}
\]

Averaging (4.1) against \(\mu\) gives zero. Hence

\[
q_\mu(x)=0
\qquad\text{for }\mu\text{-almost every }x.
\tag{4.2}
\]

Every support point is thus a minimum of the smooth function \(U_\mu\) on
the sphere. It follows that

\[
\nabla_{S^2}U_\mu(x)=0
\tag{4.3}
\]

and

\[
\operatorname{Hess}_{S^2}U_\mu(x)\succeq0
\tag{4.4}
\]

on the support.

These are Karush–Kuhn–Tucker, or **KKT**, conditions: necessary conditions
for a constrained optimum. Here the constraints are positivity and total
mass one for the measure, together with the spherical constraint on point
positions.

For \(s=x\cdot y\),

\[
\nabla_{S^2}U_\mu(x)
=
\int K'(s)(y-sx)\,d\mu(y).
\tag{4.5}
\]

For a tangent vector \(v\perp x\),

\[
\operatorname{Hess}_{S^2}U_\mu(x)[v,v]
=
\int
\left[
K''(s)(v\cdot y)^2-sK'(s)\lVert v\rVert^2
\right]d\mu(y).
\tag{4.6}
\]

Equations (4.1)–(4.6) are exact analytic facts; they do not depend on an
atomic approximation.

## 5. Flags and flag squares

Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be independent samples from \(\mu\). Every polynomial
in their pairwise inner products can be represented by a multigraph:

- a sampled point is a vertex;
- a factor \((X_i\cdot X_j)^m\) is an edge \(ij\) of multiplicity \(m\).

The expectation of such a monomial is a **moment label**.

A **rooted flag** keeps some sampled points fixed as roots and averages over
the remaining points. For example, with roots \(X_1,X_2,X_3\),

\[
\Phi_{\mathbf e}(X_1,X_2,X_3)
=
\int\prod_{i=1}^3(X_i\cdot Y)^{e_i}\,d\mu(Y).
\]

For any real coefficients \(c_{\mathbf e}\),

\[
\mathbb E\left[
\left(
\sum_{\mathbf e}c_{\mathbf e}\Phi_{\mathbf e}(X_1,X_2,X_3)
\right)^2
\right]\ge0.
\tag{5.1}
\]

Expanding (5.1) introduces two independent leaves \(Y,Z\) sharing the same
roots. It therefore becomes a positive semidefinite constraint on moments of
five sampled points.

This is the flag-algebra mechanism used here: square a conditionally averaged
rooted expression, then forget the roots by averaging. It is analogous to
Razborov's flag-algebra method, with Gram monomials replacing finite graph
densities.

The **arity** is the number of sampled vertices appearing after a square is
expanded. The **degree** is the total polynomial degree in Gram entries.
Increasing degree enriches the functions available on a fixed configuration.
Increasing arity adds genuinely new multipoint correlations.

## 6. Root-weighted flags

The strongest current block also permits factors among the roots:

\[
\Phi_{\mathbf r,\mathbf e}(X_1,\ldots,X_s)
=
\left(
\prod_{i<j}(X_i\cdot X_j)^{r_{ij}}
\right)
\int
\prod_i(X_i\cdot Y)^{e_i}\,d\mu(Y).
\tag{6.1}
\]

Flags are separated according to their parity under \(X_i\mapsto-X_i\).
Only flags with the same parity character are paired in a Gram matrix. This
ensures that the unrooted square is well defined on projective space.

## 7. Why “KKT-infused”

Ordinary flag squares use only expressions such as (5.1). The KKT conditions
provide additional nonnegative expressions:

\[
\int \rho_\mu(z)q_\mu(z)\,d\sigma(z)\ge0
\qquad\text{if }\rho_\mu(z)\ge0,
\tag{7.1}
\]

and

\[
\int
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(
B_\mu(x)\operatorname{Hess}_{S^2}U_\mu(x)
\right)d\mu(x)\ge0
\qquad\text{if }B_\mu(x)\succeq0.
\tag{7.2}
\]

The multipliers \(\rho_\mu\) and \(B_\mu\) are themselves constructed from
rooted flag squares. The equalities (4.2) and (4.3) may be multiplied by
arbitrary signed flag expressions because their averages remain zero.

Thus a KKT-infused flag certificate combines:

\[
\text{ordinary flag squares}
+
\text{PSD multipliers of the Hessian}
+
\text{nonnegative multipliers of the global gap}
+
\text{free multipliers of KKT equalities}.
\]

Every term is justified either by a square, a KKT inequality, or an exact
identity.

## 8. Exact geometric identities

The code also uses:

1. **Antipodal symmetry.** A monomial with odd total degree at a sampled
   vertex has zero expectation.
2. **Isotropic contraction.** A vertex of degree two can be integrated using
   \(\int xx^T\,d\mu(x)=I/3\).
3. **Rank-three Gram identities.** Four vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) are
   linearly dependent, so

   \[
   \det\operatorname{Gram}(X_1,X_2,X_3,X_4)=0.
   \]

   The identity remains zero after multiplication by any Gram polynomial and
   averaging.
4. **Exchangeability.** Relabeling independent samples does not change an
   expectation. Graph labels are therefore canonically identified up to
   vertex permutation.

## 9. From squares to an SDP

Choose finite bases of flags. Give each family an unknown symmetric Gram
matrix \(Q\). Requiring \(Q\succeq0\) makes the associated quadratic form a
sum of squares. Expand every block in the common basis of moment labels.

The primal SDP asks for PSD matrices and free KKT/identity multipliers such
that every moment coefficient equals the corresponding coefficient of
\(T\). The dual SDP asks for a formal moment functional satisfying every
positivity and equality constraint while minimizing \(T\).

A negative dual optimum means the chosen finite hierarchy has not certified
zero. A value close to zero is not an exact proof unless a primal
decomposition is recovered and checked exactly.

## 10. Why a scalar magic function is insufficient

A classical two-point magic-function proof would seek a scalar zonal
function \(h\le K\) with nonnegative harmonic coefficients. Such a sharp
function cannot handle the full equality family here.

For example, take mass \(1/3\) at a pole and mass \(2/3\) uniformly on its
equator. This measure has zero energy. Its equatorial pair inner products
fill \([-1,1]\). Equality in a pointwise minorant proof would force
\(h(t)=K(t)\) on the entire interval, but \(K\) has a negative degree-four
harmonic coefficient.

The analogue of a magic function must therefore be a rooted, matrix-valued,
higher-point positive kernel rather than a single scalar function of one
inner product.
