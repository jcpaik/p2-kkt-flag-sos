# Cohomological approaches to the Hankel relaxation

## Is there a cohomological explanation?

Yes—though probably representation-theoretic or Koszul/BGG cohomology, rather than ordinary topological cohomology.

There is a striking differential interpretation. Fix \(x\in S^2\) and define

\[
f_x(y)=(x\cdot y)^3.
\]

Writing \(t=x\cdot y\), spherical differentiation gives

\[
|\nabla f_x|^2=9t^4(1-t^2),
\]

and

\[
\left|(\nabla^2 f_x)_0\right|^2
   =18t^2(1-t^2)^2,
\]

where \((\nabla^2f)_0\) is the trace-free Hessian. Consequently,

\[
B=\frac19\widetilde{\mathbb E}_{x,y}|\nabla f_x(y)|^2,
\qquad
A=\frac1{18}\widetilde{\mathbb E}_{x,y}
       |(\nabla^2f_x(y))_0|^2.
\]

Thus the missing inequality

\[
B\le A+\frac14C
\]

is a weighted Weitzenböck/Poincaré inequality:

\[
2\|\nabla f\|_H^2
\le
\|(\nabla^2f)_0\|_H^2+\frac92C.
\]

Now the operator

\[
D:f\longmapsto(\nabla^2f)_0
\]

is a first BGG-type operator on the sphere. Its kernel consists of the low spherical harmonics

\[
\ker D=\mathcal H_0\oplus\mathcal H_1.
\]

Since \(f_x\) is odd, only the \(\mathcal H_1\) obstruction occurs. The cubic decomposes as

\[
t^3=\frac25P_3(t)+\frac35P_1(t).
\]

The correction

\[
C=\left\|H_1-\frac13I\right\|^2
\]

looks precisely like the squared size of that low-harmonic obstruction after averaging in \(x\). So the desired inequality has the expected form

\[
\text{coercivity of }D
\quad+\quad
\text{norm of its cohomology class}.
\]

There is also an algebraic version. On \(\mathbb P^2\times\mathbb P^2\), the three components of

\[
x\times y
\]

generate the ideal of the diagonal \(x\parallel y\). The two feature families are

\[
t^2(x\times y)_i
\]

and

\[
t(x\times y)_i(x\times y)_j.
\]

Multiplication by \(x\times y\), together with the syzygies

\[
x\cdot(x\times y)=y\cdot(x\times y)=0,
\]

belongs naturally to the Koszul/Eagon–Northcott resolution of the diagonal ideal. The failure of that multiplication map to be coercive should be measured by a finite-dimensional cohomology group. Representation theory suggests that the surviving component is spin \(2\), exactly the representation containing \(H_1-I/3\).

So a plausible proof program is:

1. Construct the finite polynomial complex generated by \(x\times y\), restricted to bidegree \((3,3)\).

2. Equip it with the pseudo-inner product induced by \(H\otimes H\).

3. Establish a Hodge decomposition for the two feature spaces.

4. Show that the only harmonic/cohomological component is the spin-\(2\) tensor \(H_1-I/3\).

5. Compute the smallest nonzero singular value of the boundary map. The claim predicts that its squared constant is exactly \(1/4\).

The real difficulty is that the inner product comes from an arbitrary Hankel \(H\), not Haar measure. Standard integration by parts therefore does not apply automatically. The crucial question is whether full six-index Hankel symmetry supplies the required formal-adjoint identities algebraically. If it does, this would turn the numerical phenomenon into a genuine finite-dimensional Hodge theorem.

## Execution of the proposed proof program

The outcome is negative: the analogy is real, but the ordinary cohomological machinery does not prove the Hankel inequality. The obstruction appears exactly at the formal-adjoint step.

For \(t=x\cdot y\), \(c=x\times y\), and \(f_x(y)=t^3\),

\[
|\nabla f_x|^2=9t^4(1-t^2),\qquad
|(\nabla^2 f_x)_0|^2=18t^2(1-t^2)^2.
\]

Thus

\[
B=\frac19\|\nabla f\|_H^2,\qquad
A=\frac1{18}\|(\nabla^2f)_0\|_H^2.
\]

For Haar measure, the trace-free Hessian is a BGG operator satisfying

\[
D^*D=\frac12(-\Delta)(-\Delta-2),
\]

whose kernel is \(\mathcal H_0\oplus\mathcal H_1\). But this identity uses integration by parts. A Hankel functional only gives

\[
\ell(pq)=\ell(qp)
\]

and positivity \(\ell(p^2)\ge0\); it does not give an integration-by-parts identity for derivatives. Hence the Haar adjoint \(D^*\) is not the adjoint for the \(H\)-dependent pseudo-inner product.

The algebraic replacement also fails to provide the sign. The diagonal ideal in

\[
R=\mathbb R[x_1,x_2,x_3,y_1,y_2,y_3]
\]

has the Hilbert–Burch resolution

\[
0\longrightarrow R(-3)^2
 \xrightarrow{\,[x\;\;y],}
 R(-2)^3
 \xrightarrow{\,c^T\,}
 I_\Delta
 \longrightarrow0,
\]

because \(x\cdot c=y\cdot c=0\). Put \(v=tc\). Then exactly

\[
A=\|c\otimes v\|_H^2,\qquad B=\|tv\|_H^2.
\]

But the associated “Hodge Laplacian” gives only

\[
\|c\otimes v\|^2-\|tv\|^2
  =\bigl(|c|^2-t^2\bigr)|v|^2
  =(1-2t^2)|v|^2,
\]

which is sign-indefinite. The Koszul exactness explains the two SOS quantities \(A\) and \(B\), but it does not compare them.

The correction

\[
C=\left\|H_1-\frac13I\right\|^2
\]

is global—it uses the fact that the two copies carry the same Hankel functional. It is not generated automatically by the cohomology of the diagonal resolution. Producing the sharp coefficient \(1/4\) would require a genuinely new weighted Hodge inequality

\[
\boxed{\;
\|tv\|_H^2
\le \|c\otimes v\|_H^2
   +\frac14\left\|H_1-\frac13I\right\|^2 .
\;}
\]

There is a legitimate cohomological object behind a singular Hankel matrix: its apolar Artinian Gorenstein algebra. However, positivity of the middle catalecticant does not automatically imply Hodge–Riemann relations. Indeed, the Hankel cone has higher-rank extreme rays constructed through positive Gorenstein ideals and Cayley–Bacharach configurations, as studied by [Blekherman and Sinn](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.1873). Those are precisely the pseudo-moment states that prevent the standard cohomological argument from reducing to a measure argument.

So the execution identifies a precise failure:

\[
\text{Hankel symmetry}
\;\not\Rightarrow\;
\text{BGG integration by parts}.
\]

A cohomological proof remains possible only if one proves a new Hodge–Riemann theorem for positive ternary socle-six Gorenstein functionals whose degree-three pairing is PSD. In this case that new theorem would essentially be the missing inequality itself, rather than a consequence of the existing Koszul/BGG complex. This program therefore does not yet produce the requested proof.
